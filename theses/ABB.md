One parcel, no additions, nothing sold. $263,000 in, about $12,800 of dividends

years. Solidly good and not spectacular, which is what a share-gain story looks
like when it works and does not re-rate.

It is also the second-largest single cheque in the ledger, and it went in on a
company one analyst covered and one relative used.

## The five-minute phone call

This is the part worth recording properly, because it is the only original
research in the file.

Before buying I rang **Thomas**, my brother, who has two houses in Australia,
and asked him a deliberately open question: have you heard of Aussie Broadband,
what are they like? He didn't hedge. They're awesome. He uses them at both
houses. All-Australian call centres. They understand the Australian psyche.
Way better than Telstra or Optus.

Five minutes, one customer, no methodology. And yet it is the most decision-
relevant thing in the whole process, because the thesis is not really about
network economics — it is about whether a household that has been on hold to
Telstra will switch and then stay. Thomas is that household, twice, and he
answered without being led.

The right way to hold that datum is the way P2 and the pre-mortem hold it: it
confirmed the mechanism, it did not measure the market. One happy customer with
no reason to disappoint me, describing a service he had already chosen, is
confirmation and not evidence. It moved the decision from "interesting" to
"buy", and that was the correct amount of weight to give it.

## The contrast with AR9 is the whole lesson

Two positions in this ledger were bought partly on the strength of a
conversation with a brother.

**AR9**: the brother was the chief technology officer. He could describe, in
real detail, why the technology was clever — attribute-based document access
control, restriction down to GPS location. Everything he said was true and none
of it was about whether anyone would buy it. The thing that killed the
investment was that the company could not sell, and a CTO is the one person in
the building who will not tell you that.

**ABB**: the brother was a customer. He could not describe the technology and
did not need to. He could tell me the one thing that mattered — that the
product is good enough that he pays for it at two addresses and would recommend
it unprompted.

Same instinct, opposite value. Asking someone inside the company gets you the
product. Asking someone who pays the bill gets you the business. AR9 is
currently the worst position in the ledger and ABB is a comfortable winner, and
this is not a coincidence.

## Why Grade B and not A

Every document underpinning this thesis existed before the money went in — all
three Intelligent Investor pieces, and the FY22 result they analyse. That is
what separates B from C: the reconstruction below is built from the actual
information set of November 2022, not from a memory of how it felt.

But there is **no contemporaneous write-up of my own**. No thesis, no valuation
work, no sizing note, no record of the phone call at the time. The same
standard applies here as everywhere: LAU is Grade A because the entry post came
six weeks after the buy; AR9 is Grade C because the decision that mattered left
no record at all. ABB sits with DRO, NHC and PWH in the middle.

## What tomorrow's result should answer

In pillar order, so the review can be written straight off the back of it:

- **P1** — where is NBN share now, and are net adds still growing? The
  million-customer target was three years out from a base just under 585,000.
- **P2** — churn and ARPU, and any language about "efficiency" near the words
  "customer service". Churn was already the soft spot in FY22, blamed on
  call-centre staffing. If the fix for margin pressure is the call centre, P2
  is breached whatever the headline says.
- **P3** — the split of EBITDA between residential, business, enterprise and
  wholesale. This is the pillar that turns a reseller into a carrier and it is
  the one with the longest runway.
- **P4** — founders still in place, directors still buying or at least not
  selling.

And the standing question underneath all four: whatever happened to the NBN
pricing review. It was described at the time as a huge development, especially
for challenger brands. Three and a half years later I could not tell you how it
landed, which is its own small indictment.
