## Why this one is different

Every other reconstructed thesis in this system leans on someone else's
analysis with me supplying the decision. Computershare is the one where I had a
real edge of my own, and it is worth stating plainly: **I was a customer for
years before I was a shareholder.**

My Brambles stock options while I worked there, and my Rolls-Royce options,
were both administered through Computershare. I dealt with the registry from the
outside as an investor and from the inside as an employee shareholder. I knew,
without needing a broker note to tell me, how unglamorous and how sticky the
service is — nobody changes registrar to save a few dollars, least of all in the
middle of a live corporate deal. That is the moat, and I had felt it.

Intelligent Investor supplied the valuation and the cyclical framing; the "know
what you own" part was, for once, literal.

## The record

Four decisions, and the first three are the ones that built the position.

- **7 Feb 2014, $11.65** — the first parcel, near the analyst's buy price.
- **17 Jan 2015, $10.04** — added below it, into the Aussie-dollar-shielded
  derating.
- **17 Jan 2016, $8.97** — added again, cheaper still. The best-timed of the
  three.
- **13 Apr 2021, $13.55** — a later top-up, well after the thesis had begun to
  prove itself.

Scaling in below $12 across 2014–2016 is the reason this became a decade-plus
multi-bagger; the ledger holds the returns.

## The bet, and why it worked

The insight in the 2013–2015 Intelligent Investor pieces was that Computershare
earned from two things that were both, at the time, at cyclical rock-bottom:
**interest on the ~$15bn of client cash it holds**, being crushed by near-zero
rates; and **corporate-actions and M&A activity**, dead since the GFC. The
market was pricing the registry as if neither ever recovered.

So the price bought the moat and threw in two coiled springs for almost
nothing, with a falling Australian dollar — Computershare reports in US dollars
— as a translation tailwind on top. You did not need all three to fire; you
needed patience.

The interest-rate spring, in particular, uncoiled with a vengeance years later,
and it is the single biggest reason the stock re-rated from around $11 to many
times that. Which is exactly why the pre-mortem and the resolution question both
insist on the uncomfortable flip side.

## Why Grade B

The documents underpinning this are contemporaneous with the buying — the 2013
picks-and-shovels piece, the December 2014 upgrade, the February 2015 interim —
and I was scaling in across exactly that window. What is missing, as everywhere,
is a written record of my *own* reasoning at the time. So B, not A, on the same
standard as the rest.

## The pillar I would defend hardest, and the one I would attack

**P2 is the whole game.** It is also where I am most exposed to fooling myself.
The thesis was that two cyclical lows would normalise, and they did — but that
means the earnings that made this a winner are, in large part, a rate cycle. The
kill condition on P2 and the resolution question both point at the same thing:
separating the durable moat and the cash returns (which compound) from the
margin income on client cash (which is a rate bet). Being right for a cyclical
reason is still being right — but only if I remember which reason it was when the
cycle turns.
