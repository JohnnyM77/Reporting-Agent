## Why this is graded A, and the one thing that is not

Everything above the line is reconstructed from posts written *at the time*, in
my own words, with dates. That is as good as evidence gets and it is why this
is the only Grade A file so far.

The exception, and it matters: **no kill condition existed in writing in
February 2024.** The pillars and their kill conditions here are a later
structuring of contemporaneous prose. So the reconstruction can see where the
lines should have been drawn, and there is an obvious hindsight risk in drawing
them exactly where the business later crossed them.

Which is the whole lesson of this file. The February 2024 post is unusually
honest about *risks* — Clay is an unknown, no operational metrics are
published, subcontractor costs grew 44% against 22% revenue. All four
pre-mortem items are genuinely contemporaneous, not retrofitted. What was
missing was not insight. It was any statement of what would make me sell.

## The two-year gap between knowing and acting

The credibility flag went up on **26 February 2024** — four days after the
thesis was published, and six weeks after the first buy. Management used
"underlying" numbers when statutory EBITDA was $7m *higher*, and the note
written that day says using a fake set of numbers for no reason instantly
lowers credibility because the assumption is that something is being hidden.

Two years later the exit post lands on management again, from the other
direction: not dishonest, just not commercial. The same management doubt,
raised in month two, resolved in month twenty-eight.

In between, HY26 in February 2026 had every fact the May exit relied on —
revenue outrunning EBITDA, leverage above comfort, soft cash conversion — and
the verdict was still "patient". The post even says so: *"I'm probably leaning
into my patient investor vibe a bit too much here."*

"Be slow to buy and even slower to sell" is good advice that becomes an excuse
the moment there is no written condition attached to it. Patience and paralysis
produce identical behaviour and feel completely different from the inside. The
only thing that separates them is a kill condition written down before the fact.

## What actually broke

P2. The claim was that scale would convert into operating leverage. Across
FY24, FY25 and H1 FY26 the pattern never changed: revenue up hard, EBITDA up
less, NPAT flat once depreciation and interest were paid. Growth was bought
with capital and delivered with cost, and the incremental margin never arrived.

P3 broke alongside it, and the two are the same failure seen from different
ends — operating leverage in a low-margin freight business *is* cost control,
and cost control is a management behaviour.

P1 never broke. The acquisitions did what they were supposed to: revenue grew,
rail doubled, the rural footprint diversified out of a ~80% Queensland
concentration to roughly 50%. Being right about the strategy and wrong about
the economics is the specific way this one lost money.

## The archetype, chosen in hindsight

Filed as `VALUE_TRAP`, which is not what I thought I was buying. At entry this
looked like a cheap compounder: 12% revenue CAGR, ROCE tripling, ten times
earnings. What it turned out to be was a low-margin business whose cheapness
was a correct assessment of its economics rather than a mispricing of them.

The alternative recorded at entry says it plainly. Mainfreight was named as the
better business with the better culture and rejected on price — 24x forward
against LAU's 10x. The quality gap was the thing that mattered and the multiple
gap was the thing I looked at.
