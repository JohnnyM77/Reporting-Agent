## Why this is filed as a cyclical trade

Because it is one, and because the archetype changes what the file is for.

A compounding machine is judged on whether the business keeps getting better.
A cyclical trade is judged on two decisions: the price you paid, and whether
you sold. Mitchell Services is a drilling contractor. It owns ninety rigs and
rents them to mining companies whose exploration budgets rise and fall with
commodity prices they do not control either. There is no version of this where
it compounds quietly for twenty years.

Filing it as `CYCLICAL_TRADE` means the resolution criterion has a second half
that most of the other files do not have: *did I act on the answer*. Being right
about the trough and still holding the same position two years into the upswing
is not a partial success. For this archetype it is the failure mode.

## What the entry case actually was

Four valuation measures, averaged to $0.72 against a share price in the
mid-thirties:

| Measure | Implied |
|---|---|
| 6x two-year average free cash flow (~$30m) | $0.84 |
| NTA off management's stated $120m fleet value | $0.57 |
| Listed peer EBITDA multiples | ~$0.70 |
| Mining-services transaction EBITDA multiples | $0.75 |

A rig-count multiple implied $0.93 and was thrown out as unreliable, which is
the detail that tells you the work was honest.

Three of those four measures are multiples applied to the earnings of a
cyclical business, and the fourth is asset backing. Averaging four numbers that
mostly share one input does not reduce the uncertainty, it launders it. The
version I would write today is shorter: the assets said around 57c, everything
else said more, and the price was 36c.

The other thing the source got right, and which deserves saying: it was written
with the explicit expectation that the *next* result would be worse. Buying a
cyclical requires being willing to look wrong for a year. Noddy74 said FY25
would be softer than FY23 or FY24 but that FY26 was prospective. FY25 NPAT came
in at $0.5m. FY26 came in at $15.2m.

## The FY26 result, and the awkward part of being right

Bob's analysis this morning: revenue up only 5.5% on a rig count that *fell*,
and EBITDA margin from 13.1% to 20.7%. NPAT $15.2m against $0.5m. Operating
cash flow $37.4m, more than double, at better than 100% pre-tax conversion. Net
debt of $8.4m became net cash of $3.5m. Return on invested capital 25% against
2%. The dividend is now fully franked, which it was not at entry.

None of that came from drilling more holes. Gold went from 47% to 61% of
revenue and steelmaking coal fell from 39% to 30%, and the mix did the work.

That is the pre-mortem, almost word for word: *"If margins recover, it will be
because the revenue mix moved toward whatever metal is in favour... Gold prices
are not my edge and never have been."*

So the position is up and the reasoning was borrowed and the driver was one I
listed as a risk rather than a reason. Being right for the reason you wrote
down as a risk is still being right. It is not evidence of skill, and the
review scores it that way — process 7, outcome 9.

## What actually needs watching now

Not the share price, and not the gold price, because neither is forecastable
from here.

- **29 of 90 rigs are idle.** This is the whole bull case and the whole bear
  case in one number. Idle rigs are free operating leverage if utilisation
  rises and unabsorbed fixed cost if it does not.
- **Top customer at 23% of revenue, up from 17%.** Concentration grew in a good
  year, which is when it always grows.
- **Capital commitments went from $1.5m to $8.6m.** Fleet spending ramping up
  after the cycle has already turned is the single most reliable way a mining
  services company gives back a good year. P2's kill condition covers it and P4
  now names it as the sell trigger.
- **Safety went backwards** while the rig count was rising off its low. Clients
  review contracts on safety records, so this is a commercial number, not only
  an ethical one.

## The one pillar that moved

P3 is now strained, and it is the pillar the entry thesis was proudest of.

The entry case cited Scott Tunbridge — Deepcore's founder, who sold the
business to Mitchell, stayed to run it, and joined the board — as one of the
little details that point to a good culture. That reading was reasonable and
may still be right.

The FY26 accounts also show around $4m of billings to entities connected to key
management: an equipment supply business connected to Tunbridge at $3.5m, plus
rent and shared legal costs to Mitchell-connected entities. Tunbridge resigned
from the board effective 30 June 2026. Everything is disclosed and described as
arm's length, and none of it is an allegation of anything.

But you cannot cite a relationship as evidence of culture on the way in and
then decline to look at it when the same relationship starts generating
supplier income. The kill condition now names related-party dealings
explicitly. That is what a TIGHTENED amendment is for.
