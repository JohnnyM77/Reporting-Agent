## A note on the ticker

This position was bought as **Woodside Petroleum (WPL)**. In June 2022 Woodside
merged with BHP's petroleum arm and renamed itself **Woodside Energy Group
(WDS)** — the same event that put a small parcel of Woodside scrip into this
portfolio via the BHP holding. The thesis lives under **WDS** because that is
what the shares are today and what the ledger tracks; everything below was
written about WPL as it was in 2017–2019.

## The record

Three decisions, and they tell the story better than the thesis does.

- **15 Feb 2019, $37.15** — the first parcel, and the expensive one. Bought
  above Intelligent Investor's buy price on the strength of Morningstar's
  higher fair value.
- **3 Mar 2020, $21.84** — the COVID oil crash. The best decision of the three
  by a distance, and the one that did the real work in the blended return.
- **25 May 2022, ~$0.01** — Woodside scrip received in the BHP petroleum merger.
  It arrived; it was not bought. Flagged as demerger scrip and carries no IRR,
  because a multiple on a near-zero cost base is a meaningless number.

The decision table on the slide pulls all three from the ledger.

## Why this position exists

Two Intelligent Investor pieces by Gaurav Sodhi supplied the idea, and it is a
genuinely good one. Woodside had spent years worried about reserve replacement
— the 2014 "We need to talk about Woodside" concern that, without reinvestment,
it would slowly decay. Sodhi's insight was that the global LNG market had
changed the question. When a new processing train costs US$15bn and a new
Browse plant US$30–40bn, nobody builds them; instead, owners of stranded gas
bring their resource to whoever already owns the infrastructure. Woodside owns a
lot of it. Its asset base stops being a maintenance burden and becomes a tolling
booth.

That is the bet: **the utility hiding inside the oil stock.**

## Why Grade B

The documents underpinning this are contemporaneous with the decision — the

## The uncomfortable part

I did not buy at the value analyst's price. Sodhi's buy line was $31–32; I paid
$37.15. What justified that was a *different* valuation — Morningstar's fair
value of $41.52 and its four-star rating — which reached higher than
Intelligent Investor's did.

That is worth naming plainly, because it is a recurring shape: when one source
says wait and another says buy, taking the one that lets you act is not the same
as having conviction. The pre-mortem keeps it on the record — *"the margin of
safety I relied on was Morningstar's higher number, not Intelligent
Investor's."*

The COVID top-up at $21.84 is the redemption, and also the tell: the price the
value analyst was actually waiting for did arrive, thirteen months later, and it
was worth $15 a share less than I first paid.

## The pillar I would defend hardest

**P1 — the real value is the infrastructure, not the oil.** It is the only
differentiated idea in the file; everything else is standard resource-cyclical
reasoning. And its kill condition is the sharpest thing here: *Woodside sanctions
a large greenfield project at full new-build cost.* The entire thesis is that
the company earns returns on existing assets with minimal capex. The day it
commits tens of billions to a new-build, it stops being the utility in the story
and becomes an ordinary capital-hungry producer — and that is exactly the
outcome the pre-mortem is watching for.
